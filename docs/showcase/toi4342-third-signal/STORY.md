# The third rhythm — TOI-4342

One star, two confirmed planets, and a third transiting candidate. Start with the question **How could a third planet hide in a star we already know?** Decompose it backward, then teach the prerequisites forward in a continuous 3D world.

## Reverse prerequisite tree

- A third planet candidate requires a repeated signal, an orbital interpretation, and checks against impostors.
- A repeated signal requires brightness, blocked area, time, and phase.
- An orbital interpretation requires circular motion, projection, and an observer's line of sight.
- Rejecting impostors requires locating the lost light, comparing separate observations, and distinguishing a detection from confirmation.

## Forward visual argument

1. A constellation of prerequisite spheres grows backward from the question and lights forward from light, time, and projection.
2. Three circular orbit curves reveal correct relative distances and angular speeds. The outer dashed curve remains a candidate. Changing the camera reveals that an elliptical projection can belong to a circle in space.
3. A close view of the spherical star and a planet with the fitted radius ratio demonstrates blocked light along one observer's sightline. The candidate's fitted impact parameter offsets the chord from the center.
4. A belt on a celestial sphere marks viewing directions that could produce transits. The actual narrow angular width follows the circular model; a separately labeled wider belt makes its surface easy to inspect. The belt's annular topology is a teaching object, not evidence of a planet.
5. Time wraps around a phase cylinder. Repeated synthetic dips line up at the same phase. Masking known b/c windows removes samples before searching; it does not subtract confirmed planets from space. No synthetic curve is represented as downloaded TESS photometry.
6. Eight sector measurements become points and uncertainty rods in the 3D stage. Sector indices are labels, not uniformly spaced times. The saved vetting output counts nine transit events; eight sectors and nine events are different quantities.
7. Return to the dashed outer orbit: a candidate worth follow-up, with Gaia-constrained model FPP about 11.1%, above the 1.5% statistical-validation threshold.

## Scientific contract

The host mass/radius are rounded to 0.63 solar masses and 0.60 solar radii from Tey et al. (2023). Circular orbit distances are calculated with Kepler's third law. The overview enlarges bodies but preserves orbital distance and period ratios. Orbital phases, surface textures, circularity, and mutual orientations are illustrative. The transit closeup uses the candidate fit k = 0.0337894 and impact parameter b = 0.746895. Raw light-curve samples are not included in the public candidate folder; synthetic illustrations are explicitly labeled. The separate 47.5-day RV candidate is not the 18.908-day transit signal.
